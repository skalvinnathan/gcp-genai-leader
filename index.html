Domain 1 · about 30% of the exam

Fundamentals of gen AI

This is the base. We start from zero: what AI is, how a machine “learns”, what a foundation model is, and what Google's four model families do. Read slowly. Every later page uses these words.

Official source for this domain: exam guide section 1 (PDF).

1. The big picture: AI, ML, deep learning, gen AI

AI, ML, deep learning, gen AI — nested boxes

AI (artificial intelligence) means machines doing things that normally need human thinking, like learning, solving problems and making decisions.

ML (machine learning) is a part of AI. Instead of a programmer writing every rule, the machine learns patterns from data.

Deep learning is a part of ML. It uses neural networks with many layers to find very complex patterns.

Generative AI (gen AI) is an application of ML that creates new content: text, images, code, audio, video.

AI ┐
   └─ Machine learning ┐
                       ├─ Deep learning
                       └─ Generative AI  (creates new content)

Daily-life picture: Think of Russian dolls. The biggest doll is AI. Inside it is ML. Inside that is gen AI.

Example: A model that says “this email is spam” is classic ML. A model that writes a new email for you is gen AI.

Remember: gen AI is smaller than ML, not bigger. Not every AI task needs gen AI. Predicting which customers will leave is still a classic ML job.

NLP — natural language processing

NLP is the part of AI that works with human language: reading, understanding and writing words. Large language models are a modern, very strong way to do NLP.

Daily-life picture: NLP is the “language department” of AI.

2. How machines learn: three approaches

Supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement

ApproachDataSimple ideaExample
SupervisedLabelledLearn from examples that come with the right answer.Emails marked “spam / not spam”. The model learns to mark new emails.
UnsupervisedUnlabelledFind groups and patterns without any answers.Group customers who behave alike.
ReinforcementNo fixed dataset; it acts and gets feedbackTry things. Get rewards for good moves and penalties for bad moves.A robot learning to move in a warehouse.

Daily-life picture: Supervised = studying with an answer key. Unsupervised = sorting a box of mixed buttons into piles by yourself. Reinforcement = training a dog with treats.

Remember the trigger words: “labelled” → supervised. “groups / patterns, no labels” → unsupervised. “rewards / penalties” → reinforcement.

3. Data: the fuel

Structured vs unstructured; labelled vs unlabelled

Structured data sits in neat rows and columns, like a table in a database or a spreadsheet. Unstructured data has no fixed shape: free text, emails, images, audio, video, PDFs. Most business data is unstructured, and gen AI is good at it.

Labelled data has a tag with the right answer (a photo tagged “cat”). Unlabelled data is raw, with no tags.

Daily-life picture: Structured = a neat filing cabinet. Unstructured = a box of mixed papers, photos and voice notes.

Data quality and data accessibility

Good data is complete (nothing missing), consistent (same thing written the same way), relevant (about your problem), and accurate. It must also be accessible: available, in a usable format, and not too costly to get.

Example: If “Bengaluru”, “Bangalore” and “BLR” all appear in your customer table, your model sees three different cities. That is a consistency problem.

Remember: “garbage in, garbage out”. A bigger model does not fix bad data.

4. The ML lifecycle

Five stages, in order

  1. Data ingestion — collect data from many places.
  2. Data preparation — clean it and shape it so a model can learn from it.
  3. Model training — the model learns from the data.
  4. Model deployment — make the model available for people and apps.
  5. Model management — look after it: versions, tracking quality, watching for drift, updates.

Daily-life picture: Opening a restaurant. Buy ingredients (ingest), wash and cut (prepare), cook the recipe (train), open the doors (deploy), keep the kitchen running and fix problems (manage).

Remember: the exam guide also asks for “Google Cloud tools for each stage”. On Agent Platform, for example, Model Garden helps you find models, Pipelines helps automate steps, Model Registry keeps versions, Model Monitoring watches for drift and Feature Store manages data features. See the catalog.

5. Foundation models, LLMs and friends

Foundation model

A foundation model is a very large model trained on a huge amount of varied data. It learns broad knowledge. You can use it for many tasks, or adapt it for one task. That is why we call it a “foundation”.

Daily-life picture: A university graduate with a general education. You can train her for many jobs, instead of raising a new person for each job.

Three key features: trained on diverse data; flexible for many use cases; can be adapted for special domains with extra training.

LLM — large language model

An LLM is a foundation model for language. It reads text and writes text. It does this by learning patterns of which words tend to follow which. It can answer questions, summarise, translate, write, and more.

Remember: all LLMs are foundation models, but not all foundation models are LLMs. (Image and video models are foundation models too.)

Multimodal model

“Modality” means a type of data: text, image, audio, video. A multimodal model can take in and/or give out more than one type.

Example: You send a photo of a broken part and ask “what is this and how do I fix it?”. The model reads the image and answers in text.

Diffusion model

A diffusion model makes images (and sometimes video) by starting from random noise and removing the noise step by step until a clear picture appears.

Daily-life picture: A foggy window that slowly clears and shows a picture.

Prompt, prompt engineering and prompt tuning

A prompt is the input you give the model, such as a question or a task. Prompt engineering is writing and improving prompts to get better answers. Prompt tuning is a light technique to adapt a model for a task without retraining all of it.

Remember: prompt engineering = a person writes better instructions (no training). Prompt tuning = a model-adaptation technique. The exam guide lists both, so know that they are different.

6. Words you must know: token, context window, temperature, hallucination

Token

Models do not read whole sentences. They cut text into small pieces called tokens. A token can be a short word or part of a long word. Cost and limits are often counted in tokens.

Context window

The context window is how much text (how many tokens) the model can think about at one time. It includes what you send and what it writes back.

Daily-life picture: The size of your desk. A small desk holds a few papers. A big desk holds a whole contract.

Example: A 300-page contract is too long for a small window. Choose a model with a bigger window, or give the model only the needed parts (this is where RAG helps; see Domain 3).

Temperature

Temperature is a setting for randomness. Low temperature: the model picks the most likely words, so answers are steady and repeatable. High temperature: the model takes more chances, so answers are more varied and creative.

Remember: facts and consistency → low. Brainstorming → higher. (More in Domain 3.)

Hallucination

A hallucination is when a model says something false or invented, in a confident voice. It happens because a model predicts likely words. It does not “check” facts.

How to reduce it: grounding, RAG, clear prompts, lower temperature, and a human reviewing important answers. (Domain 3.)

Inference

Training is when a model learns. Inference is when you use the trained model to get an answer. Every time you send a prompt, the model does inference.

7. Choosing a foundation model

Factors from the exam guide

  • Modality — does it handle the data types you need (text, image, audio, video)?
  • Context window — how much can it read at once? (Bigger can cost more.)
  • Security — does it meet your data and privacy rules?
  • Availability and reliability — will it be up when you need it, under load?
  • Cost and performance (accuracy, speed, efficiency) — there is often a trade-off.
  • Fine-tuning and customisation — can you adapt it?

Daily-life picture: Buying a vehicle. Do you need seats (modality), a big boot (context window), safety (security), a reliable engine, a price you can pay, and can you modify it?

8. What gen AI helps businesses do

Create · Summarise · Discover · Automate

  • Create new content: text, images, code, video.
  • Summarise long information into short points.
  • Discover the right information at the right time (smart search).
  • Automate tasks that were manual.

Examples: text generation (emails, reports), image generation (ad pictures), code generation (helper for programmers), video generation (short clips), data analysis (ask questions of data in plain English), personalised user experience (tailored suggestions).

9. The five layers of the gen AI landscape

Infrastructure → models → platforms → agents → applications

Applications — the part users see (a chat app, Gemini in Gmail).
Agents — software that pursues a goal and uses tools to act.
Platforms — APIs, data tools and model deployment. They hide the hard infrastructure work.
Models — the trained “brains” (Gemini, Gemma, Imagen, Veo).
Infrastructure — chips (TPUs, GPUs), servers, data centres, cloud.

Daily-life picture: A restaurant. Land and kitchen equipment (infrastructure), chefs (models), the restaurant's operating system for orders (platform), waiters who take actions (agents), the menu and the dining room the guest sees (application).

Business meaning: you can “enter” at any layer. A small team may just use a ready application. A bigger team may build on the platform. Very few build their own infrastructure.

Domain 2 · about 35% of the exam (the biggest)

Google Cloud's gen AI offerings

This is the biggest domain, and it has the most product names. Take your time. A simple way to learn it: ask “who is the user?” for each product. An employee? A customer-service team? A developer? The answer usually points to the right product.

Official source: exam guide section 2 (PDF) and Google's study guide.

The 2026 exam guide uses the new names (Agent Platform, Agent Search, Agent Studio). Older material says Vertex AI. I write both. Full table: Names in 2026.

1. Why Google Cloud? (section 2.1)

Google's five strengths

  • AI-first. Google has worked on AI for many years and keeps investing. New gen AI ideas reach products fast.
  • Enterprise-ready. Responsible, secure, private, reliable and scalable.
  • One big ecosystem. Gen AI is built into many Google products (Search, Workspace, Cloud).
  • Open approach. You get choice: Google's models, open models, and third-party models. You are not locked in.
  • AI-optimised infrastructure. Google's own TPUs (chips built for AI), GPUs, Google data centres and cloud computing. Together this is described as a “hypercomputer”.

Daily-life picture: A TPU is like an engine built only for one job, so it does that job fast. A GPU is a strong all-round engine that is also good at AI.

Two more ideas in the guide: (1) Control over your data — security, privacy and governance; you choose models and tools. (2) Democratising AI — low-code and no-code tools, pre-trained models and APIs so more people can build.

2. Ready-made AI for work (section 2.2)

Gemini app (and Gemini Advanced, Gems)

The Gemini app is Google's gen AI assistant. You chat with it to write, summarise, translate and make images. Gemini Advanced is the name the exam guide uses for the upgraded version with extra features and company-grade protections. A Gem is your own custom assistant inside Gemini, with saved instructions, like a reusable template.

Example: A marketing manager makes a Gem called “Brand voice helper”. It already knows the company tone. She does not retype the rules each time.

Note: Google changes the names of its consumer plans often. The exam guide still says “Gemini Advanced”, so learn that name. check current plan names on Google's site

Gemini Enterprise

Gemini Enterprise is Google's workplace AI product for companies. Employees can search across company data, ask questions and use custom agents. The exam guide mentions the Gemini Notebook API, multimodal search and custom agent abilities.

Daily-life picture: A very smart company librarian who can read your internal documents and apps, and also run errands (agents).

History: Press reports say Agentspace (an earlier Google product for enterprise search and agents) was folded into Gemini Enterprise. press reports; check Google's pages Gemini Enterprise is also described as the umbrella name for Google's enterprise AI.

Gemini for Google Workspace

Gemini built into the Workspace apps people already use. In Gmail it can draft emails. In Slides it can make images. In Meet it can summarise notes. In Docs and Sheets it can help write and analyse.

Who is the user? Anyone using Workspace. No setup needed to build anything.

Gemini Notebook (NotebookLM)

You upload your own sources (documents, links). It becomes a research assistant that summarises, answers questions and makes ideas — and it stays grounded in your sources. It can also make an audio overview.

Name: Google's current study guide calls it Gemini Notebook. The website address is still notebooklm.google.com, and many people say NotebookLM. Learn both. name as used in Google's 2026 materials

Gemini for Google Cloud

An AI assistant for people who build and run things on Google Cloud. It helps write and debug code, manage and improve cloud apps, analyse data in BigQuery and strengthen security. Gemini Code Assist is the coding-helper side. Code Assist is not named in the exam guide

4. For developers: Agent Platform (section 2.4)

Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (Agent Platform)

The main Google Cloud platform to build, tune, deploy and manage AI models and agents. It is the new name that grew out of Vertex AI. It gives you models (Gemini and others) and lets you tune them, deploy them and watch them.

Daily-life picture: A big workshop with tools, a store of ready parts (Model Garden), and a loading dock to ship your finished product.

Parts to know:

  • Model Garden — a catalog. Pick from Google, third-party and open-source models.
  • Model builder / training — train your own models. Fully custom with an ML framework, or AutoML to build models with little ML skill.
  • Agent Studio (formerly Vertex AI Studio) — try prompts and build production-ready gen AI apps at scale.
  • Agent Search — prebuilt search and RAG.
  • Agent Runtime (formerly Vertex AI Agent Engine) — where deployed agents run. from the release notes; not named in the exam guide
  • MLOps tools — Pipelines, Model Registry, Model Monitoring, Feature Store: to automate, version, watch and manage.

RAG offerings on Google Cloud

Prebuilt RAG with Agent Search = easiest. Google handles the retrieval for you. RAG APIs = building blocks if you want to control each step. (RAG itself is explained in Domain 3.)

Custom agents

If a ready product is not enough, developers can build their own agents on Agent Platform, with their own tools, data and rules. See the next section for what an agent is.

5. Agents and their tools (section 2.5)

What is an agent?

A gen AI agent is software that tries to reach a goal by observing what is going on and acting with the tools it has. A normal chatbot mostly answers. An agent can do things: look up data, call another system, start a task.

Three parts: a model (the brain), a reasoning loop (observe → think → act, repeat), and tools (ways to touch the outside world).

Three types: deterministic (fixed paths, like a flowchart), generative (talks in natural language using an LLM), and hybrid (both, which is powerful).

Daily-life picture: A chatbot is a person at a help desk who only talks. An agent is a personal assistant who also books the ticket, checks your calendar and sends the email.

Domain 3 · about 20% of the exam

Techniques to improve gen AI output

Models are smart but not perfect. They make things up, they are out of date, and they can be unfair. This domain is about what you can do about it: better prompts, connecting the model to real facts, extra training, human review, and watching the model over time.

Official source: exam guide section 3 (PDF).

1. Limits of foundation models (3.1)

Six limits to know

LimitPlain meaning
Data dependencyThe model is only as good as its training data. Bad or missing data → bad output.
Knowledge cutoffThe model knows nothing after its training data stops. Like a book printed on one day.
BiasUnfair leaning learned from data. Small biases can grow in the output.
FairnessDoes the model treat groups of people fairly? Must be checked.
HallucinationA confident answer that is false or invented.
Edge casesRare, odd situations the model did not see enough, so it fails.

Fixes: which tool for which problem?

ProblemGood first fixWhy
Model invents facts or lacks your company dataGrounding / RAGGive it real documents to answer from.
Answers are vague or in the wrong formatPrompt engineeringCheapest and fastest. No training.
Needs a lasting special style or skill for a narrow taskFine-tuningExtra training on task examples.
High-risk or sensitive answersHuman in the loop (HITL)A person checks before it matters.
Quality drops over timeMonitoring and evaluationTrack metrics, spot drift, update.

Remember the order of effort: prompt engineering (easy) → grounding / RAG (medium) → fine-tuning (more work). Try the cheap fix first.

Human in the loop (HITL)

People review or give feedback on the model's work, inside the workflow. It matters most for content moderation, sensitive fields (healthcare, finance) and high-risk decisions. A human can check before (pre-generation: review the setup and data) and after (post-generation: review outputs and give feedback).

Fine-tuning, in simple words

Fine-tuning means taking a pre-trained model and training it a little more on your own examples, so it behaves better for your task. A related, cheaper idea is parameter-efficient tuning: change only a small part of the model. the exam guide names “fine-tuning”; parameter-efficient is extra background

Daily-life picture: A chef who already cooks many cuisines. You give her two weeks to learn your restaurant's exact recipes.

Monitoring and evaluation

A model in production must be watched. Good practice from the guide: versioning (Model Registry), performance tracking and KPIs (key performance indicators), drift monitoring (Model Monitoring), security patches and updates, automatic model upgrades, and Feature Store to manage data features. Pipelines can automate the steps.

Drift = the model slowly gets worse because the real world or data changed.

Evaluation = test with the same set of questions and agreed measures, and also use human review, because gen AI answers are free text.

2. Prompt engineering (3.2)

Why prompts matter

The model does what the prompt says — and only what it says. A clear, specific prompt (task, context, format, tone) usually gives a better answer than a vague one.

Vague
“Write about our product.”
Better
“You are a friendly sales writer. Write a 3-sentence product blurb for busy managers about our expense app. Mention saving time. End with a question.”

Prompting techniques (guide 3.2)

TechniqueWhat you doExample
Zero-shotAsk with no examples.“Translate to French: Good morning.”
One-shotGive one example.“Happy → positive. The food was cold → ?”
Few-shotGive several examples.Three sample emails, then “write another”.
Role promptingGive the model a persona.“You are a tax advisor…”
Prompt chainingWork step by step in a conversation, each step builds on the last.Outline → expand section 2 → shorten.

Daily-life picture: Teaching a new colleague. Zero-shot: “just do it”. One-shot: “do it like this one”. Few-shot: “here are three samples”.

Advanced prompting

  • Chain-of-thought (CoT) — guide the model to reason step by step (often by showing examples with the steps). Good for maths and logic.
  • ReAct (Reason + Act) — the model alternates between reasoning and taking actions (like calling a search tool), then reasons again with the result. This is how many agents work.
  • Metaprompting — using prompts to write, change or read other prompts. from Google's study guide

3. Grounding and RAG (3.3)

Grounding

Grounding connects the model's answers to real information you can check. It fights hallucination and the knowledge cutoff. The guide names three kinds of data to ground with:

  • First-party data — your own enterprise data (policies, product docs, tickets).
  • Third-party data — data from other providers.
  • World data — broad public knowledge, such as the web.

Daily-life picture: “Show your sources.” A student who writes an essay with a bibliography is grounded.

RAG — retrieval-augmented generation

RAG is the most common way to ground a model on your data. Three steps:

  1. Retrieval — find the most relevant information from outside sources.
  2. Augmentation — add that information to the prompt.
  3. Generation — the model writes the answer using it. (It can also repeat the loop if needed.)

Daily-life picture: An open-book exam. You do not memorise the book (no retraining). You look up the right page, then answer.

Example: An employee asks “How many leave days do I get?”. The system retrieves the current HR policy page, adds it to the prompt, and the model answers from that page.

Why leaders like RAG: answers use fresh data; the model needs no retraining when documents change; you can show sources.

Business strategies for a successful gen AI solution

The smallest domain, but it is the “leader” part of the title. It asks: how do you choose, start, secure and measure a gen AI project, and how do you do it responsibly? Many answers here are common sense. Good news.

Official source: exam guide section 4 (PDF) and Google's study guide.

1. Implementing a gen AI solution (4.1)

Types of gen AI solutions

Text generation, image generation, code generation, and personalised experiences for users. Pick the type that matches the business problem.

Choosing the right solution: needs and resources

Before you start, ask these questions (from Google's study guide):

Needs
  • Scale — how many users?
  • Customisation — how special must the AI be?
  • User interaction — how will people use it?
  • Privacy — how sensitive is the data?
  • Latency — how fast must it answer?
  • Connectivity — is the network reliable?
Resources
  • People — do you have AI skills?
  • Money — what is the budget?
  • Time — what is the deadline?

Daily-life picture: Buy, rent or build a house? Depends on need (family size), money, skills and time. Gen AI: use a ready product, adapt a model, or build custom — and the more custom, the more money, skills and time.

Rule of thumb: common need + small team + short time → a ready-made product. Very special need + strong team + budget → customise or build on the platform.

A good strategy (top-down + bottom-up)

  • Top-down: leaders set the vision and strategy and give resources.
  • Bottom-up: employees find practical uses and give feedback.
  • Strategic focus: pick focused uses with clear business value.
  • Exploration: let people experiment.
  • Responsible AI: ethics and security from day one.
  • Resourcing: invest in data strategy, reuse what you have, grow AI talent.
  • Impact: measure results against business goals.
  • Continuous improvement: keep refining from feedback and data.

Integrating gen AI and managing change

Technology alone does not create value. People must use it. Steps that help: explain why, involve staff early, train them, start with a pilot, collect feedback, then grow. This is called change management.

Example: A bank pilots a meeting-summary tool with one team for six weeks, measures time saved, fixes problems, then rolls it out to other teams.

Measuring impact

Choose clear goals before you start (time saved, cost saved, quality, customer satisfaction, revenue). Measure a baseline (before). Track the same numbers after. Report results. No measurement = no proof.

2. Secure AI (4.2)

What is secure AI?

Protecting AI systems, their data and models from attacks and misuse. Security is needed across the whole ML lifecycle: data, training, deployment and monitoring.

SAIF — Secure AI Framework

SAIF is Google's framework that helps organisations manage the security risks of AI and ML models and build secure AI systems. Think of it as Google's checklist of good security habits for AI.

3. Responsible AI (4.3)

What is responsible AI?

Making sure AI does not cause harm and is used in an ethical way. Think about it through the whole life of the system. The guide asks for these ideas:

  • Transparency — be open about when and how AI is used and what its limits are.
  • Privacy — protect personal data. Know the risks.
    • Anonymisation — remove identity so a person cannot be found again.
    • Pseudonymisation — replace identity with a code. The link can be restored with a separate key, so it is weaker.
  • Data quality, bias and fairness — bad or unbalanced data makes unfair results.
  • Accountability — a clear owner is responsible for outcomes.
  • Explainability — you can explain why the system gave an answer.

Example: A loan model rejects more people from one area because the old data was unfair. A responsible team tests for bias, fixes the data, explains decisions, keeps a human reviewer and monitors results.

Where people lose marks

Traps: confusing pairs

Exam writers like to put two similar things in the answers. Here are the pairs that confuse most people, and the one-line way to tell them apart.

RAG vs fine-tuning

RAG = give the model the right documents at question time (open book). Fine-tuning = train the model more on examples so its behaviour changes (study before the exam).

Tie-breaker: data changes often or must be fresh/private → RAG. A lasting style or narrow skill → fine-tuning. Try prompts first, then RAG, then fine-tuning.

Grounding vs prompt engineering

Prompt engineering changes how you ask. Grounding changes what facts the model can see. A perfect prompt cannot give the model facts it does not have.

Tie-breaker: “made-up facts / old data” → grounding. “unclear or wrong format” → prompt.

Grounding vs RAG

Grounding is the goal (tie answers to real sources). RAG is one common way to do it. Grounding with Google Search is another way.

Google AI Studio vs Agent Studio (formerly Vertex AI Studio)

AI Studio = free, quick prototyping with the Gemini API. Agent Studio = enterprise, production-ready apps at scale on Google Cloud.

Tie-breaker: “prototype / free / quick” → AI Studio. “production / governance / scale” → Agent Studio.

Vertex AI vs Agent Platform

Not two products. Agent Platform (Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform) is the 2026 name that grew out of Vertex AI. If both appear in the answers, one is probably an old-name version of the other.

Gemini vs Gemini Enterprise vs Gemini app

Gemini = the model family (the engine). Gemini app = chat assistant for people. Gemini Enterprise = workplace product for company data and agents. Gemini for Workspace = Gemini inside Gmail, Docs…

Gemma vs Gemini

Gemma is open, small and for developers to run and customise. Gemini is Google's flagship multimodal family. Gemma is not “Gemini lite”.

Imagen vs Veo

Imagen = images. Veo = video.

Agent Assist vs Conversational Agents

Conversational Agents talk to customers. Agent Assist helps human agents who talk to customers.

Speech-to-Text vs Text-to-Speech

Say the direction out loud: Speech → Text (transcribe) and Text → Speech (read aloud).

Translation vs Document Translation

Document Translation keeps the layout of formatted documents. Plain Translation is for text and similar content.

Cloud Vision vs Cloud Video Intelligence vs Document AI

Vision = pictures. Video Intelligence = video. Document AI = forms and invoices (extract fields).

Temperature vs top-p vs safety settings

Temperature = randomness. Top-p = size of the word pool. Safety settings = filter harmful content. None of them adds facts.

Context window vs knowledge cutoff

Context window = how much text fits right now. Knowledge cutoff = the date when the model's training knowledge ends.

Few-shot vs fine-tuning

Few-shot puts examples inside the prompt (no training, temporary). Fine-tuning trains the model on examples (lasting change).

Prompt engineering vs prompt tuning

Prompt engineering: a person writes better prompts. Prompt tuning: a light model-adaptation technique.

Anonymisation vs pseudonymisation

Anonymisation removes identity for good. Pseudonymisation uses a code and can be reversed with a key.

Chatbot vs agent

A chatbot mostly talks. An agent pursues a goal and uses tools to act.

Training vs inference

Training = the model learns. Inference = you use the trained model to get an answer.

Gen AI vs ML

Gen AI is a part of ML that creates content. Prediction jobs like churn can still use classic ML.

Prebuilt RAG (Agent Search) vs RAG APIs

Prebuilt = less work, less control. APIs = more work, more control.

IAM vs Security Command Center

IAM = who can access what. Security Command Center = see your security posture and risks.

General exam traps

  • “Always” and “never” are usually wrong. Real life has exceptions.
  • Business over technology. This is a leader exam. The best answer usually fits the business need, the risk, and the cost.
  • Cheapest working fix first. If a prompt change solves it, do not choose fine-tuning.
  • Responsible and secure answers beat fast and risky ones when both are possible.
  • Read the last line of the question. “Which is the MOST…”, “FIRST step”, “BEST”.
  • Old names. If a name looks unfamiliar, ask what its job is. It may be a renamed product.

Practical advice

Using the path and exam-day tips

How to use the learning path well

  • Order: do the 5 courses in order (see the path page).
  • Active, not passive. After each video, close it and say the main points out loud in your own words. If you cannot, watch that part again.
  • Use the tools. Open the Gemini app, make a Gem, and try Gemini Notebook with a few of your own documents. Try Google AI Studio with a prompt. Ten minutes of play teaches more than an hour of reading.
  • Use this site for a second explanation. If a course word is hard, find it in the glossary.
  • Take notes by the exam's four domains. Not by course. The exam does not care which course taught you.
  • Take Google's sample questions once, near the end. They are free, English only, not timed. Google says they do not predict your score. (Link on the sources page.)
  • English is your second language? Learn the exam words, not only the ideas: grounding, hallucination, token, context window, prompt, fine-tune, drift, bias, fairness, accountability. Use the flashcards.

Before exam day

  • Book early and choose: online-proctored (at home) or a test centre. Read the exam rules and system checks for online testing on Pearson VUE's pages (links on the sources page).
  • Name on your ID must match the name on your booking. Check this days before, not on the morning.
  • Online exam: clear desk, quiet room, stable internet, camera and microphone working, valid ID. Run the system test the day before.
  • Two days before: do the quiz in “wrong answers only” mode. Read the traps page.
  • Night before: read the cheat sheet once, then sleep. Sleep beats cramming.

During the exam (90 minutes, 50–60 questions)

  1. Read the last sentence first — what is it asking? “BEST”, “FIRST”, “MOST”.
  2. Find the user and the problem. Employee? Customer? Developer? Made-up facts? Too slow? Too risky?
  3. Cross out wrong answers. Usually two are easy to remove. Then compare the last two.
  4. Prefer the simplest fix that works. Prompt first, then RAG, then fine-tuning.
  5. Prefer responsible and secure answers when a question mentions customers, health, money or personal data.
  6. Unknown product name? Think: “is it an old name?” Then match by job (see Names in 2026).
  7. Pace: about 1.5 minutes per question. If stuck, pick your best guess, mark it, and move on. Use any spare time to review marked questions.
  8. Do not leave blanks. (Check the exam instructions on the day; I could not verify whether wrong answers lose marks, but multiple-choice exams of this kind usually do not subtract.) scoring rules unverified

After the exam

  • Results and next steps come from Google and its test provider. Follow their emails and your certification dashboard. timing unverified
